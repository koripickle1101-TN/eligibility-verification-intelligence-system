Eligibility Verification Intelligence System

I built EVIS to study what can happen when inaccurate or incomplete information enters the workflow before eligibility verification—and how a small front-end defect can become a much more visible problem later.

As I work toward my Bachelor's of Science degree in Healthcare Administration, I use simulated projects like this one to practice patient access, eligibility workflow analysis, control design, synthetic data analysis, and root-cause thinking without presenting student work as professional healthcare employment.

Integrity boundary

Student-developed. Simulated. No PHI.

No patient information, protected health information, employer data, payer data, claims data, EHR data, or real eligibility transactions are used. All rates, rework counts, and comparison outcomes are modeled educational values—not real-world healthcare performance results.

Patient-to-professional perspective

From the patient side, an eligibility problem may not feel like “data quality.” It may feel like another phone call, a delayed appointment, a reschedule, uncertainty about coverage, or having to repeat information. That is why I wanted to look earlier in the workflow and ask where the issue actually started.

01

Why I Built EVIS

I wanted to practice looking past the visible eligibility exception and tracing the problem back to the first point where inaccurate or incomplete information entered the workflow.

What I am studying

The difference between a failure and a signal

An eligibility exception can be the first visible sign that something is wrong, but it may not be where the problem began. EVIS helps me practice separating the original failure from the signal that appears later.

Why that matters

Front-end problems can travel downstream

If a demographic or insurance issue is not caught early, the next person in the workflow may inherit the problem. The patient may experience the consequences even though the original defect happened much earlier.

04

Control Design

I modeled a simple workflow that separates prevention, detection, correction, and a gate that keeps an unresolved exception from moving forward unnoticed.

01

Structured verification

Preventive control. Review critical demographic and insurance information before eligibility verification.

02

Eligibility verification

Detective control. Treat a failed or unexpected response as a signal that needs investigation.

03

Exception gate

Workflow control. Keep an unresolved exception from moving quietly into later workflow steps.

04

Correct + reverify

Corrective control. Validate the source data, correct the simulated defect, and recheck before appropriate downstream progression.

Important: This is a student-designed control model for learning. It is not presented as a validated healthcare intervention or a production workflow.

New EVIS control module

Detection ≠ Resolution

EVIS now extends the exception gate with an Eligibility Exception Ownership & Closure Control™. A detected issue does not become “resolved” simply because someone found it. The modeled workflow requires documentation, clear ownership, action, escalation when needed, and verified closure before progression.

Required workflow Detect → Review Evidence → Assign → Act → Verify → Close → Advance

A completed checkpoint is not the same as a controlled workflow state. EVIS now explicitly separates detection from resolution, handoff from ownership, advancement from readiness, and closure from assumption.

07

What I Learned

The exception was not necessarily the original problem. It was evidence that the workflow may have lost control earlier.

I started by looking at the eligibility mismatch as the problem to correct. Building this simulation pushed me to separate the failure, the signal, and the control—and to ask what entered the workflow earlier that made the visible exception possible.
FailureIncorrect demographic data
SignalEligibility exception
ControlVerify, investigate, resolve

That distinction matters to me because the patient usually experiences the downstream effect, not the internal label for the failure. What may look like a data-quality issue inside the workflow can feel like delay, uncertainty, or repeated work from the patient side.

New learning added to EVIS

Finding the problem is not the same as closing it.

The first control failure may not be the original eligibility problem. It may occur after the problem was correctly identified but before anyone ensured that it was documented, owned, acted on, and verified as resolved.
